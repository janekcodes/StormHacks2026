# E3 · Engelbart's demo

## Research

Douglas Engelbart and his team at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) demonstrated the oNLine System (NLS) live on December 9, 1968, at the Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco. The ninety-minute session is remembered as the "Mother of All Demos". It showed, in one working system, the computer mouse, windows, hypertext links and collaborative real-time editing, plus live video conferencing.

The mouse predates the demo. Engelbart conceived the idea in the early 1960s. The first prototype, a wooden shell with a single button and two perpendicular wheels, was built by the engineer Bill English in 1964. The demo was the mouse's first public showing.

## Narration draft

On December ninth, nineteen sixty-eight, Douglas Engelbart showed the mouse, windows, hypertext links and live shared editing in one ninety minute demo in San Francisco. Many parts of how you use a computer today appeared on that stage. Move the pointer and click a link to jump between notes, while a second cursor types beside you.

## Portal spec

- Interaction, in one sentence: Move the pointer and click a hypertext link to jump between linked notes, while a second cursor types alongside you.
- Inputs: mouse (move, click), keyboard (Tab and arrow keys to move focus, Enter to activate), touch (tap).
- States: four linked notes (the demo, the mouse, windows, collaboration), a current note, a jump history breadcrumb, and a partner cursor that types "Hello from Menlo Park" character by character.
- What the visitor learns: the 1968 demo put the mouse, windows, hypertext links and live collaboration into one system, and showed them publicly for the first time.
- Accessibility: every link is a real button, focus is visible, keyboard and touch are equivalent to the mouse, text contrast is above 4.5:1, and reduced motion shows the partner line in full immediately.

## Caveat

The label "first" applies to the December 9, 1968 demonstration as the first public showing of the mouse and of these capabilities together. The mouse itself was a 1964 prototype built by Bill English, and earlier pointing devices existed.
